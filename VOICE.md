# Voice

How every word on this site should sound. `DESIGN.md` covers how things look; this file covers how they read. Draft from these patterns at the source. Writing generic copy and sanding the AI tells off afterward produces text that is clean but still does not sound like a person.

## Who is talking

Julienna, about her own work, in the first person. "I built the ad generator", "my pipelines", "email me". Never "she", "her", or "Jules does". Her name appears only as identity: the nav lockup, page titles, and the operator node in the hero graph.

The register is a designer talking to a hiring manager she respects: complete sentences, contractions welcome, plain professional vocabulary, no slang. The warmth comes from what she chooses to say (what she likes, what a project taught her, who it was for), never from exclamation points or chatty filler.

## Six patterns to write from

1. **Tie every fact to a consequence.** A number on its own is trivia. Say what it did: "Around 40 practices each need eight ad files per campaign, so I built the generator to start every job from rules instead of a blank artboard."
2. **Source what you know; mark what you don't.** Resume numbers ship plain. Estimates say so ("Estimate, verify"). Anything unconfirmed gets the dashed placeholder chip. Never write past the evidence.
3. **Earn the confident claim.** A strong line is fine once the sentence before it has shown why. The hero can say "a whole design team's output" because the next sentence and the graph show the roles.
4. **Concession, then position.** Grant a clause, then take the stance: "Claude helps draft new templates, but fixed rules render the ads."
5. **One dry aside per section, at most.** A small, true observation that sounds like someone who has done the work. It never tips into jokes.
6. **Use her own words where they exist.** Lines from Julienna's earlier drafts are the best source of personality on the site: "I like work where design decisions become reusable systems instead of one-off files", "a soft spot for projects that care about community", "Let's build something reusable."

## Banned patterns

These are the recognizable fingerprints of model-written copy. Rewrite the sentence, do not patch the word.

- **Dashes.** No em dashes or en dashes anywhere. Restructure with a comma, colon, period, or parentheses. Ranges use a hyphen (2019-2022).
- **Triads and parallel anaphora.** "AI drafts. Rules decide. I direct." / "One inbox, one person, and one team." Collapse into a single sentence that says the thing.
- **"X, not Y" and "Y over X" framing** in headlines and principles ("Instrumentation, not decoration"). State the positive rule plainly.
- **Stock phrases.** Intensifiers that add nothing ("real work", "actually", "genuinely", "truly"); spatial metaphors for abstractions ("lives in", "sits", "under the hood", "the backbone of"); verdict idioms ("does the heavy lifting", "moves the needle", "table stakes"); knowingness markers ("worth noting", "here's the thing", "the kicker"). Name the action or the number instead.
- **Figurative stand-ins for mechanics.** If a literal verb names what happens, use it. "Fixed rules render the ads" beats "rules do the talking".
- **Marketing verbs.** streamline, empower, supercharge, leverage, unleash, transform, seamless, elevate, cutting-edge, world-class, next-gen, game-changer.
- **All-caps eyebrow labels above section headings.** The heading carries the section. Small labels are sentence-case sans (the `.meta` class); mono appears only for literal file names, paths, and code, never as a label.
- **Overpromising.** Copy matches what the work or the site does. No guarantees ("never drifts off brand"), no invented outcomes, no implied headcount.

## Length

Match the surface. Headlines stay at eight words or fewer and read as a sentence a person would say out loud. Section intros stay under about 25 words. Project summaries stay to one sentence. The personality is in the choice of detail, not the word count.

## Before and after

**AI section heading**
- Before: "AI drafts. Rules decide. I direct."
- After: "I use AI for the repetitive half"
- Why: the triad is the most recognizable model cadence there is. The rewrite makes one claim, in her voice, and the section body explains which half.

**Pipeline intro**
- Before: "Two pipelines that ship real work. My ad generator and photo intake line, step by step: one input goes in, a finished on-brand library comes out."
- After: "Two pipelines I built, and what they produce. Around 40 practices each need eight ad files per campaign, so I built these to start every job from rules instead of a blank artboard."
- Why: "real" is filler, and the colon-plus-parallel structure is decoration. The rewrite gives the reason the tools exist, using verified numbers.

**Closing call to action**
- Before: "Put a system behind your brand. One inbox, one person, and the output of a whole team."
- After: "Let's build something reusable. If you're hiring for brand, web, or creative direction, I'd like to hear about it."
- Why: her own line replaces an imperative slogan, and the anaphora becomes a plain invitation.
